# Finding the metal machines at MIT's SHED

**Short answer:** The MIT SHED (Safety, Health, Environmental Discovery lab) is a makerspace and research lab run by **MIT Environment, Health & Safety (EHS)** on the **4th floor of Building N52** (265 Massachusetts Ave). Its official capabilities page lists **metal 3D printers, 4-axis and 5-axis CNC mills, lathes, waterjets, and wire EDM**, along with FDM, SLS, DLP, and PolyJet printers, laser cutters, and vacuum formers. The page lists categories only, with no makes or models. The SHED says it is open to "the broader MIT community," but no SHED-specific eligibility rules, fees, or onboarding steps turned up. The only documented way in is **mentor-hosted open hours and scheduled trainings posted on the MIT Making LibCal "EHS The SHED" calendar**, and first-years can also enter through M1T sessions held there. So **no, you should not expect to walk in and use the machines whenever you want**: nothing points to 24/7 or unsupervised access, and the metal printer, waterjet, 5-axis mill, and wire EDM are the kinds of tools MIT shops usually keep staff- or mentor-operated. If the SHED's metal equipment turns out to be off-limits, the realistic fallbacks are the **Edgerton 6C Student Shop** for CNC (free after a 12-hour training), **The Deep** for waterjet, the **Hobby Shop** (paid membership), and, for metal printing, **a research connection to MIT's APT lab** or **an outside DMLS service** like Xometry or Protolabs. One limitation applies to everything below: this research environment's network blocked all `*.mit.edu` pages, so every finding comes from search-engine snippets of official pages, not from reading them in full. Confirm hours, eligibility, and metal-printer rules directly with the SHED before you plan a project around them.

## The SHED is an EHS research lab, not a typical student shop

Searching for "MIT SHED" mostly returns results for **RIT's SHED** (Rochester Institute of Technology's Student Hall for Exploration and Development, opened 2023), which has its own detailed public equipment documentation ([RIT SHED Makerspaces](https://www.rit.edu/shed/makerspaces)). None of that applies to MIT. MIT's SHED is a different, smaller thing: "a makerspace managed by MIT EHS, accelerating safe and broad access to advanced technologies" ([MIT Making LibCal, "EHS The SHED"](https://project-manus.libcal.com/calendar/shed)). It describes itself as "a multidisciplinary laboratory dedicated to prototyping, fabrication, and scientific discovery at MIT, offering equitable access to advanced technologies for the broader community" ([About the SHED](https://shed.mit.edu/about-the-shed)). Its founding director and principal investigator is **Dr. Tolga Durak**, Managing Director of EHS ([Tolga Durak, MIT NSE](https://nse.mit.edu/people/tolga-durak/)).

The SHED's EHS ownership affects how you get in. To reach it, you enter N52 from the 265 Mass Ave / Front St entrance, go up to the 4th floor, walk into the **EHS office and lab area**, and continue to the SHED ([LibCal M1T event at the SHED](https://project-manus.libcal.com/event/13637298)). The SHED also does research on making itself, across biology, chemistry, prototyping/fabrication, and radiation/nuclear ([shed.mit.edu](https://shed.mit.edu/?page_id=6)). One example is "Making the Makers," a longitudinal study of maker training that it runs with Project Manus founder Marty Culpepper ([Making the Makers](https://shed.mit.edu/page/making-the-makers)). The SHED is **separate from** the Project Manus / MAD Making spaces (Metropolis and The Deep) and from the **MAD N52 Fabrication Shop** in the same building. It is connected to them, though: it posts events on the shared MIT Making LibCal and hosts sessions of M1T, the first-year maker training ([LibCal event 14128285](https://project-manus.libcal.com/event/14128285)).

## Getting in runs through open hours, mentors, and LibCal

The SHED doesn't post a step-by-step onboarding procedure, but its own description is clear about who runs access: "Student and researcher mentors are the SHED's community leaders, from **designing trainings to hosting events and open hours**" ([MIT SHED](https://shed.mit.edu/)). Those trainings and open hours appear on the **[LibCal "EHS The SHED" calendar](https://project-manus.libcal.com/calendar/shed)**. **First-year undergraduates** have the most concrete route: M1T is free, open to all first-years including transfers, and some sessions are held at the SHED. These include a Zoetrope project (4 hours) and a Lixie Clock project (two sessions) that use the laser cutter, 3D printer, and soldering ([M1T FAQ](https://make.mit.edu/makerlodge/faq); [LibCal Zoetrope event](https://project-manus.libcal.com/event/13637298)). Finishing M1T also gets you access to Metropolis and The Deep, plus **$50 in Makerbucks** ([M1T](https://make.mit.edu/m1t)).

For everyone else, the best guess is that the SHED follows the general MIT shop pattern. First, complete the online Atlas safety courses ("Intro to MIT Shop Safety Rules" and "Hazard Communication"). Then attend an in-person orientation or training, have a mentor or staff member check you out on specific tools, and get space access from the facility manager once your training is verified ([Project Manus Access Resources](https://project-manus.mit.edu/making-at-mit/access-resources.html); [EHS Shops and Makerspaces](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/shops-and-makerspaces/)). Across MIT, tool credentials have been recorded in **Mobius** and are now listed on **make.mit.edu** ([Mobius Credentials](https://project-manus.mit.edu/mobius/mobius-credentials.html); [MIT MAKE](https://make.mit.edu/)). Whether the SHED uses that system is unconfirmed. On eligibility, the "broader MIT community" language and the mention of "student and researcher mentors" suggest that upper-level undergrads and grad students are welcome. No fee schedule was found, and no evidence of a fee either.

On the "whenever I want" question, the evidence points to no. The only access model on record is **mentor-hosted open hours plus scheduled events**. The space sits inside an EHS office suite, and **no source mentions 24/7 or tap-card access** to the SHED. Some other MIT spaces do offer 24/7 card access by request, and Project Manus mentors get off-hours access as a perk ([Become a Mentor](https://make.mit.edu/mentor)). If you want more time on the machines, becoming a SHED mentor is probably the best way to get it, but that is an inference, not a published policy.

## The equipment list is serious, but it names no models

The SHED's [Capabilities page](https://shed.mit.edu/page/capabilities) divides its tools into two areas. The **prototyping space** has hand tools, electronics, digital design, and "additive manufacturing through a suite of **FDM / SLS / DLP / PolyJet / Metal 3D printers**." The **fabrication space** "enables CNC machining and subtractive manufacturing by **4-axis & 5-axis mills, lathes, waterjets, vacuum formers, laser cutters & engravers and wire EDM**." Beyond fabrication, there are a wet-chemistry lab with fume hoods (including a perchloric-acid hood), a biofabrication suite with bioreactors and 3D bioprinters ([Integrated Biofabrication](https://shed.mit.edu/page/integrated-biofabrication)), and an industrial-hygiene testing lab ([shed.mit.edu](https://shed.mit.edu/)).

For metalwork, the 5-axis mill and the wire EDM stand out. Neither is common in MIT's open student shops, and together with a metal printer they make the SHED one of the more capable metal facilities on campus *on paper*. The catch is in what no source says. **No page names the metal printer**, and none says whether a student can run it, submit a job to it, or reach it only through a research project. The SHED's general promise is that "mentors are prepared to support users in the intricacies of every equipment, tool and process" ([MIT SHED](https://shed.mit.edu/)). Still, metal additive manufacturing involves fine reactive powders, inert gas, and sintering furnaces. An EHS-run lab is very likely to treat it, along with the waterjet, 5-axis mill, and EDM, as **staff- or mentor-operated equipment**, not something you can use on your own after a quick checkout. Your access is only confirmed for the laser cutter, FDM printing, and soldering, because first-years already use them in M1T. Also, the Capabilities page has no date, so the list may not reflect October 2026.

## Other MIT routes to CNC and metal printing

If the SHED's metal tools are restricted, MIT has dependable alternatives for **CNC**. Metal **3D printing** is much harder to find. The **Edgerton 6C Student Shop** (6C-006A) has manual and CNC mills and lathes plus a CNC machining center. After **12 hours of evening training** (offered monthly, and ending with a steel flashlight you machine yourself), "MIT students from across campus" can use it for research and personal projects ([MIT News, 2020](https://news.mit.edu/2020/edgerton-center-celebrates-reopening-student-shop-0204); [Edgerton 6C training](https://edgerton.mit.edu/for-MIT-students/student-shops/edgerton-6c-student-shop/training)). **The Deep** (37-072), run by MAD Making (formerly Project Manus), offers milling, turning, and a **waterjet** to all MIT community members ([Project Manus "Our System"](https://project-manus.mit.edu/thedeep-metropolis.html)). The new Met Warehouse, which opened in 2026, includes "a campus-wide makerspace run by Project Manus," so check whether any of these spaces have moved ([MIT News, Aug 2026](https://news.mit.edu/2026/met-warehouse-opens-new-home-mit-school-architecture-planning-0812)).

Several of these pages are years old, so check current rules.

For **metal 3D printing**, no open-access student metal printer or metal print-job service turned up anywhere at MIT other than the SHED's unconfirmed listing. The confirmed metal additive equipment belongs to **APT (Additive Processing and Technologies)**, John Hart's research facility within LMP. It has an **EOS M100 laser powder-bed fusion system** and a **Desktop Metal/ExOne InnoventX binder jetter** ([APT](https://apt.mit.edu/)). LMP requires a **cost object** (a research or department account) for its printers and waterjet ([lmp-shop.mit.edu](https://lmp-shop.mit.edu/)), which in practice means a UROP, a research lab, or a related class. MIT's campus print service printed PLA only and **shut down in December 2023**. It now points users to an outsourcing list that includes **Xometry** and **Protolabs** ([3D Printing Service](https://project-manus.mit.edu/making-at-mit/3d-printing-service.html)), and both sell DMLS metal printing ([Xometry](https://www.xometry.com/capabilities/3d-printing-service/); [Protolabs DMLS](https://www.protolabs.com/services/3d-printing/direct-metal-laser-sintering/)). For a single personal metal part, an outside DMLS service is the most dependable option today.

| Current equipment categories | [shed.mit.edu/page/capabilities](https://shed.mit.edu/page/capabilities) and [Spaces](https://shed.mit.edu/spaces) |
| Safety prerequisites | Atlas shop-safety courses; [EHS Shops and Makerspaces](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/shops-and-makerspaces/) |

When you email the SHED, ask four specific questions. What metal printer do you have, and does a student run it, submit jobs, or get access only through research? Are the 5-axis mill, waterjet, and wire EDM available to trained students or operated by staff? Is there any access outside mentor-hosted open hours? Who pays for metal powder and machine time?

## Conclusion

The SHED stands out for its *equipment breadth*: few MIT spaces list metal printing, 5-axis milling, and wire EDM together. Its EHS identity is likely to set its *access style*. A lab whose stated mission is to study and expand **safe** access to advanced tools is likely to offer those tools through supervised, mentor-led sessions, possibly tied to research, and not as walk-in self-service. If you're serious about metalwork, approach the SHED as a collaborator. Show up to open hours, ask about mentoring or research involvement, and frame your metal project as something the lab can learn from.

Meanwhile, don't wait on the SHED for your CNC skills. The Edgerton 6C training is the most reliable way to get hands-on CNC milling and turning with no affiliation required, and it will make any later SHED checkout easier. For metal printing, keep a budget line for Xometry or Protolabs until the SHED or APT confirms a student route. The SHED's metal printer may well be the only student-reachable one on campus, but no source confirms that yet.
